Which Portuguese words have no English equivalent?

There is no exact English word for saudade, cafuné or jeitinho. Each names something English can only describe in a phrase, and each is Brazilian Portuguese, not a poetic invention. Below are accurate, non-romanticised explanations with real examples, plus the mistranslations that make learners sound wrong.

By Renata · November 6, 2026 · 6 min

Saudade: missing something you may never get back

Saudade is the feeling of missing someone or something that is absent, often with the knowledge that the absence may last. It is not just 'I miss you' at the airport. You can feel saudade of a place, a period of your life, a person who died, or even a food you cannot find abroad. The word implies a gap between what was and what is.

English 'missing' is close but flatter. 'I miss my mother' reports a fact; 'Estou com saudade da minha mãe' carries the weight of the distance. The plural saudades is common in Brazil: 'Saudades de você' is warm and natural, not dramatic. Do not translate it as 'nostalgia', which in Portuguese usually points to a cultural or historical mood, not a personal absence.

  • Estou com saudade do Brasil. = I miss Brazil (and feel the absence).
  • Que saudade da minha infância! = I feel such longing for my childhood.
  • Saudades! = a common, friendly message to someone far away.

Cafuné: the physical gesture, not a mood

Cafuné is the act of running your fingers gently through someone's hair or stroking their head, usually to soothe or show affection. It is a concrete gesture, not an abstract feeling. A mother gives a child cafuné; a couple gives each other cafuné on the sofa. English has no single verb for it, so you say 'stroking someone's hair', which sounds clinical or vague.

The word is Brazilian and everyday, not literary. 'Fazer cafuné' is the usual collocation. Note that it is not the same as a massage or a scalp treatment; it is intimate and casual. If you translate it as 'caress', you lose the specific location and motion, and you may make it sound more romantic than it is.

  • Ela fez cafuné no filho até ele dormir. = She stroked her son's hair until he fell asleep.
  • Adoro receber cafuné. = I love having my hair stroked.
  • Cafuné is a noun; the verb is fazer cafuné, not cafunar in everyday speech.

Jeitinho: the workaround, with a warning

Jeitinho is a small, clever workaround used to solve a problem or get around a rule. It can be innocent, like fixing a broken shelf with wire, or ethically questionable, like finding a way to skip a queue. The full phrase jeitinho brasileiro often carries a knowing smile, but it is not automatically a compliment. Brazilians themselves debate whether it is creativity or corruption.

English 'workaround' or 'hack' covers the practical side but misses the social meaning. A jeitinho usually involves people, improvisation and a bit of charm. 'Dar um jeitinho' means to find a way, often when the official path is blocked. Do not translate it as 'solution', which is neutral and too clean.

Other Brazilian words English flattens

Desenrolar is the verb that often goes with jeitinho: it means to untangle a problem, to get things moving. 'Deixa que eu desenrolo' means 'leave it to me, I'll sort it out'. English 'sort out' is close, but desenrolar suggests a process with twists, not a single fix. Acolhimento is another: it is the act of receiving someone with warmth and practical support, not just 'welcoming' them at a door.

Then there is apego, which is attachment but often with a positive, affectionate tone in Brazil, and fofoca, which is gossip but can be light and social. None of these are untranslatable in the sense of impossible; they are untranslatable in the sense that a single English word loses the everyday texture. That is why learners need examples, not dictionary glosses.

  • Desenrola isso para mim. = Sort this out for me.
  • Ela é muito acolhedora. = She makes people feel genuinely cared for.
  • Fofoca boa é aquela que a gente ouve sem culpa. = a common joke, not a definition.

European Portuguese would give you different answers

Some lists online mix Brazilian and European Portuguese, which is a problem. Cafuné is Brazilian and everyday in Brazil; in Portugal it is understood but not used the same way. Jeitinho is also strongly Brazilian in its cultural weight. If you learn these words from a European Portuguese source, you may get the spelling right and the usage wrong.

This matters for exams too. The CIPLE exam tests European Portuguese and marks Brazilian usage as incorrect. So if your goal is that exam, these words are not the priority. If your goal is to understand Brazilians, they are essential. I teach Brazilian Portuguese only, online, to English speakers, and I do not prepare anyone for CIPLE for exactly that reason.

How to learn these words without romanticising them

Do not memorise them as poetry. Learn them in short, ordinary sentences: 'Estou com saudade', 'Faz cafuné nela', 'Dá um jeitinho'. Notice who says them and in what tone. Saudade from a friend is warm; jeitinho from a boss can be a warning. The meaning lives in the situation, not in the word alone.

Common questions

Is saudade really untranslatable?

It is not impossible to translate, but no single English word carries the same weight. 'I miss you' is the closest everyday equivalent, yet it does not imply the same awareness of absence. In Brazil, 'Estou com saudade' is common and plain, not poetic.

What does cafuné mean exactly?

Cafuné is the act of stroking someone's hair or head gently to soothe or show affection. It is a physical gesture, not a mood. English would say 'stroking someone's hair', which is accurate but loses the everyday intimacy of the Portuguese noun.

Is jeitinho a positive or negative word?

Both, depending on context. It can mean a clever, harmless workaround or a shady way of bending rules. Brazilians use it with a smile, but also criticise it. Do not assume it is always a compliment.

Are these words used in Portugal too?

Some are understood in Portugal, but usage differs. Cafuné and jeitinho are strongly Brazilian in everyday life. European Portuguese has its own expressions, and a Brazilian teacher will not prepare you for the CIPLE exam, which tests European Portuguese and marks Brazilian usage wrong.

Do I need to learn these words to speak Brazilian Portuguese?

You can survive without them, but you will miss tone and humour. Saudade, cafuné and jeitinho appear in ordinary conversation, songs and messages. Learning them in short sentences is more useful than memorising definitions.
