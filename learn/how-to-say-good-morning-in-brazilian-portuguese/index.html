How Do You Say Good Morning in Brazilian Portuguese?

Bom dia is how you say good morning in Brazilian Portuguese. It is the standard greeting from the moment someone wakes up until around midday. Brazilians also use boa tarde in the afternoon and boa noite in the evening and at night, and the exact cut-off between them is looser than most textbooks admit.

By Renata · October 2, 2026 · 7 min

Bom dia: the greeting you need first

Bom dia literally means "good day", but in Brazil it works as good morning. Use it when you arrive somewhere in the morning, when you greet a neighbour, a shopkeeper, a colleague or a receptionist. The word dia is masculine, which is why it is bom and not boa. That is a spelling and grammar detail worth getting right from day one.

In Brazil, bom dia is usually a complete greeting on its own. You can say it, smile, and move on. If you want to add a question, you can say Tudo bem? right after, which means something like "everything good?" and is the most common follow-up in the country.

  • Bom dia, tudo bem? Good morning, everything good?
  • Bom dia, como vai? Good morning, how are you?
  • Bom dia, senhor / senhora, Good morning, sir / madam (polite, common with older people)

Boa tarde and boa noite: the other two greetings

Boa tarde means good afternoon. Brazilians use it from around midday until it starts getting dark, which in most of Brazil means roughly until 6pm. Tarde is feminine, so it is boa, not bom. This is the same pattern as boa noite, which is feminine too.

Boa noite is the one that confuses English speakers most, because it covers both "good evening" and "good night". You say it when you arrive somewhere after dark, and you also say it when you leave or when you go to bed. Context does the work: arriving at a restaurant at 8pm, boa noite is a greeting; leaving your friend's house at 11pm, it is a goodbye.

  • Boa tarde, tudo bem? Good afternoon, everything good?
  • Boa noite, prazer em conhecer você, Good evening, nice to meet you
  • Boa noite, durma bem, Good night, sleep well

Where the cut-off really sits in Brazil

The textbook answer is that bom dia runs until noon, boa tarde from noon to 6pm, and boa noite after 6pm. In real Brazilian life, the switch is not a clock. It follows daylight, weather and local habit. In the northeast, where the sun sets earlier, you may hear boa noite from 5:30pm. In the south in summer, when it stays light until 8pm, boa tarde can still be going at 7pm.

The safest rule for a learner is this: if the sun is up and it feels like morning, say bom dia. If it feels like afternoon, say boa tarde. Once it is dark, or once people are heading home for the evening, say boa noite. Nobody in Brazil will correct you for being an hour early or late. Using the wrong one is a tiny mistake, not a rude one.

What not to say: European Portuguese traps

If you search for these greetings online, many results are European Portuguese, and some of them will not sound natural in Brazil. The greetings bom dia, boa tarde and boa noite are the same in both countries, so those are safe. The differences appear in the follow-up phrases and in how people address each other.

In Portugal, you will hear phrases and forms of address that Brazilians simply do not use in everyday speech. Brazil also uses você far more than Portugal does, and Brazilian pronunciation is very different, especially the way we say the letter t and the final sounds of words. If you are learning Brazilian Portuguese, learn it from Brazilian material, because European Portuguese resources will teach you a rhythm and vocabulary that Brazilians do not use.

Small pronunciation details that make you sound Brazilian

In Brazilian Portuguese, the word dia sounds like "jee-ah" in most of the country, not "dee-ah". The letter t before an i or a final e often becomes a soft "ch" sound. So boa tarde sounds closer to "boa tar-jee" in Rio and much of the southeast, while in parts of the south and northeast it stays harder. Both are correct Brazilian pronunciation.

The word noite ends with a sound that English speakers often turn into "noy-tee". In Brazil it is usually "noy-chee", with the same soft t. And bom dia has a nasal sound in bom that does not exist in English. Do not worry about perfecting it on day one. Brazilians are used to foreign accents and will understand you immediately.

  • dia, often "JEE-ah" in Brazil
  • tarde, often "TAR-jee"
  • noite, often "NOY-chee"
  • bom, nasal vowel, no direct English equivalent

Common questions

Is it bom dia or boa dia?

It is always bom dia. The word dia is masculine in Portuguese, so it takes the masculine adjective bom. Boa dia is grammatically wrong and Brazilians will notice it immediately. The same logic gives boa tarde and boa noite, because tarde and noite are feminine.

What time do Brazilians stop saying bom dia?

There is no fixed clock. Most Brazilians switch to boa tarde around midday, but the real trigger is daylight and habit. In some regions you will hear boa tarde from 11:30am, and in others bom dia can still be heard at 12:30pm. If you are unsure, follow what the people around you are saying.

Can I use boa noite to say goodbye?

Yes. Boa noite works both as a greeting when you arrive somewhere after dark and as a goodbye when you leave in the evening or go to bed. This is different from English, where "good evening" is only a greeting and "good night" is mainly a farewell. Context makes the meaning clear.

Is bom dia the same in Portugal and Brazil?

The phrase itself is the same in both countries. The differences show up in pronunciation, in follow-up phrases, and in forms of address. Brazilian Portuguese uses você far more, and Brazilian pronunciation of dia, tarde and noite is noticeably different from European Portuguese. If you are learning Brazilian Portuguese, use Brazilian material.

How do I reply when someone says bom dia to me?

The simplest reply is to say bom dia back. If you want to be a little warmer, add Tudo bem? or Tudo bom? after it. A common exchange is: Bom dia, tudo bem? followed by Tudo bem, e você? This is natural, friendly and used everywhere in Brazil.
