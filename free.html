Brazilian vs European Portuguese.

This is the question almost every learner gets stuck on, and most answers online are one paragraph long. Here is the whole thing: what actually differs, how much it matters, and which one to pick. Read it all here — there is no form in the way.

The short answer

One language, two standards, and a bigger gap in sound than in writing.

Portuguese is one language. A Brazilian and a Portuguese person talk to each other without adjusting anything, the way an Australian and a Canadian do. The written language is very close; since the 1990 orthographic agreement it is closer still.

The gap that matters to a learner is in the sound, and it is wider than the British-American comparison suggests. That single fact drives most of the practical advice on this page.

Difference 1 — the big one

European Portuguese eats its vowels.

In European Portuguese, vowels in unstressed syllables are reduced heavily and sometimes disappear altogether. Pequeno (small) has three clear syllables in Brazil; in Lisbon the first one shrinks to almost nothing. Telefone loses shape in the middle. Words run into each other, and the whole language sounds fast and closed.

Brazilian Portuguese keeps those vowels open and clear. Every syllable gets its moment.

This is not an aesthetic judgement — both are beautiful and neither is more correct. It is a practical point about learning: a beginner's central problem is working out where one word stops and the next starts. Open vowels make that dramatically easier. It is the main reason learners who start with Brazilian tend to reach the point of understanding real speech sooner.

Difference 2

Three grammar habits you will notice immediately.

Ongoing actions

Brazil uses the gerund: estou fazendo — I am doing. Portugal uses estar a plus the infinitive: estou a fazer. Both are correct Portuguese; they are simply the normal choice in each country. This is the single most recognisable marker of which variety someone learned.

Where the pronoun goes

Brazilians naturally put it before the verb: me diga, te amo. Portuguese writing and speech attach it after: diga-me, amo-te. Brazilian formal writing follows the older rule too, which is why this trips people up in exams but almost never in conversation.

How you address people

In most of Brazil, você is the everyday word for "you". In Portugal, tu is the normal informal form and você can sound distant or even brusque, with formal situations using a title or the person's name instead. This one is social rather than grammatical, and worth knowing before you travel.

Difference 4

The words that cause genuine embarrassment.

A short list, handled plainly, because finding out the hard way is worse. None of these makes you look foolish — they are accidents of vocabulary that catch native speakers crossing the Atlantic too.

rapariga

In Portugal this simply means girl or young woman, and is entirely neutral — teachers and newsreaders use it. In parts of Brazil it carries a strongly pejorative sense. If you learned Brazilian Portuguese and move to Portugal, this is the word that will startle you first, for no reason at all.

propina

In Portugal, the fee you pay for a university course. It is an official, everyday term. In Brazil, propina means a bribe. Portuguese universities are not asking you for a kickback.

durex

In Brazil, a brand of sticky tape so common that it became the word for it. In Portugal and much of Europe, Durex is a brand of condoms. Ask for fita-cola in Portugal, and durex only in Brazil.

bicha

An older Portuguese word for a queue, still heard. In Brazil it is a homophobic slur. The advice is the same in both directions: say fila. If you hear it in a café in Lisbon, it is about the queue.

So which one?

Pick Brazilian, unless one specific thing applies.

Choose Brazilian if you are learning for travel, for family, for work, for culture, or simply because you like the sound of it. It is roughly four fifths of the world's Portuguese speakers, it is far easier to hear as a beginner, and the supply of music, series and video to learn from is effectively unlimited. It will serve you in Portugal too.

Choose European if you are taking the CIPLE exam for Portuguese citizenship, which tests European Portuguese specifically and marks down Brazilian vocabulary in the written section — or if you already live in Portugal and your priority is understanding the person at the counter rather than being understood by them.

And if you picked one and later want the other: you are not starting again. You are learning a set of vocabulary swaps, one grammar habit, and some ear training. Weeks, not years.
