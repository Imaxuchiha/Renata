What this actually is

Not document translation. A person in the room.

Most of the trouble people run into after moving to Portugal is not legal, it is conversational. The letting agent talks quickly, the clerk asks for a document nobody mentioned, the school secretary explains a form that has no English version. None of that needs a certified translator. It needs someone standing next to you who speaks the local language natively and can tell you, in Chinese, what has just been said and what they want from you.

Viewings and letting agents

I come to the viewing, ask the questions people forget to ask — what is included, what the condominium costs, how long the contract runs, what happens to the deposit — and tell you afterwards what I would want in writing before signing anything.

Counters and appointments

Finanças for a NIF, the parish council, the town hall, the water and electricity companies, an immigration appointment. I explain what is being asked, check that your answer landed the way you meant it, and write down what you are supposed to bring next time.

Schools and after-school

Enrolment, the meeting with the teacher, the sports club sign-up, the endless forms. Parents usually cope fine with daily life and get stuck exactly here, because school Portuguese is its own dialect of bureaucracy.

Clinics and everyday admin

Booking at the reception desk, explaining a symptom, understanding what the follow-up is. Also the small things that eat a week: a repair, a delivery that never came, a bill nobody can explain. I do not interpret medical procedures or anything a hospital would want a certified interpreter for.

The honest part

My Chinese is good. It is not a court interpreter's.

HSK 4, score 210. I studied in Hubei and competed in the Chinese Bridge language competition. That is a solid everyday level: enough to explain what a clerk wants, to catch it when an answer went wrong, and to keep a conversation moving instead of dying at the counter. It is not conference or legal interpreting, and I will not pretend it is.

If a job needs a sworn translator, I will tell you that in the first message rather than after you have paid me.

What I do have that a translation agency usually does not: Portuguese as a first language, so the local side of the conversation is effortless, and English as a working language, so we always have a fallback when my Chinese runs out. Between the three of them, we get through the appointment.

Quick answers

What people ask before booking.

Can you translate official or legal documents?

No. Sworn translation, contracts, court work and medical documents are not offered here, and Portuguese authorities usually require a certified translator for them anyway. What is offered is spoken help in the room.

How good is your Chinese, honestly?

HSK 4, score 210, plus study in Hubei and the Chinese Bridge competition. Solid everyday level: enough to explain what is being asked and to catch a misunderstanding. Not a conference or legal interpreting level.

Which languages, in which direction?

Portuguese native, English fluent, Chinese as the bridge. In practice: Portuguese to Chinese, Portuguese to English, English to Chinese — whichever combination gets us through the appointment.

How far in advance should I ask?

A few days if you can. I teach in the evenings, so I take a small number of jobs and the good slots go first. If it is urgent, still write — sometimes it works out.

Do you go outside Lisbon, Oeiras and Cascais?

Sometimes, with transport at cost on top of the hourly rate. Sintra and Amadora are usually fine. Ask and I will give you a straight yes or no.

Is machine translation not enough?

Often it is, and I will say so. Where it reliably fails is formality, Portugal-versus-Brazil vocabulary and rental terms like caução or condomínio. I wrote that out in Portuguese here, with the phrases worth carrying on your phone.

Can you also teach me Portuguese?

That is the main job, yes — Brazilian Portuguese, in the evening, online or in person. Lessons and prices are here. Teaching happens in English, not in Chinese.
